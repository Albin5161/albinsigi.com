A concept project, not made or approved by LinkedIn. I copied its look so the idea can be judged inside the product.

In 30 seconds

  • The problem: After asking for a referral in a LinkedIn message, the person asking can't tell if it was seen, is being worked on, or was forgotten.
  • What I learned: Referral requests sent through LinkedIn messages can leave the requester uncertain about what happens next. A job seeker described the constant waiting as the worst part.
  • The idea: Give each referral request a status both people can see. The referrer picks from three ready answers. After seven days with no answer, an assumed wait, the system says there is no reply yet.
  • What I can claim: A working prototype that four testers finished without help. It is a concept, so there is no launch data.

Problem Identification (what's broken and why)

On LinkedIn, asking for a referral often means sending a message and waiting. You can't tell whether the person has seen your request, is working on it, or has forgotten it.

Following up can feel awkward for the person asking, and saying no can feel uncomfortable for the person asked. I explored whether a shared status could make the process clearer for both.

Why I Chose This Product

Referrals already happen inside LinkedIn, as plain messages, so a fix needs no new place to go.

Referrals are part of professional networking, which is what LinkedIn is for. A status makes a request easier to track, and better visibility could make these exchanges more predictable for both people. Whether that creates measurable value for LinkedIn is untested. It is a possible benefit, not a result.

Design Goals & Constraints

Make referral requests easier to track, cut unnecessary follow-ups, and give both people a clearer sense of where things stand.

  • The status does the reminding, not a button.
  • It lives where the ask already happens.
  • Saying no needs no writing.

Limit: LinkedIn can't see inside a company's hiring, so the referrer sets the status.

Key Design Changes & Rationale

Five decisions, each with what it costs.

DecisionWhyWhat it costs
No nudge buttonA visible status should answer the question a follow-up asks. A button would make chasing easier, not unnecessary.In one interview, the follow-up was what reminded the referrer. Without it a forgetful referrer gets no push. Whether one light reminder helps is still open.
The referrer sets the statusLinkedIn can't see inside a company's hiring.The status is self-reported. It is only as good as the referrer's last tap.
The system sets No reply yetSilence should not look like progress. After seven days at Sent with no answer, the request says so, stays open, and any later answer replaces it.It can read as a judgement of the referrer. The wording and the seven-day wait are untested.
Looking into it is its own statusIt separates seen and being worked on from never opened, which a message can't do.It could give false hope if it never moves. It keeps its last-updated date on screen, but I have no data on whether that is enough.
A ready-made no, shown in greyThere was no small way to say not this time. LinkedIn never uses red, so grey means closed.The no can arrive with no reason, unless the referrer adds the optional note.
  • Sent means sent. Nothing says Sent until it is really saved.
  • Only real news moves. Just Referred gets its own moment.
  • The ask is written for you. Strangers get checked on their resume, so one is required.

Before vs. After

Three things changed after I built it and used it.

Usability Testing (Iteration)

Four people ran the prototype start to finish on their own phones, with nobody guiding them. All four finished without help and said they would use it.

That shows the flow can be understood and completed. It does not show that real referrers would update a status, because each tester played both sides.

The test also had a flaw. The guide lit up only one way to ask, so early testers all went the same way. Halfway through I lit up both ways and recorded each choice.

Limitations & Honest Caveats

  • Six interviews show patterns, not proof. Most people were early-career, in India, in design or tech.
  • Whether real referrers would keep a status up to date is untested.
  • No reply yet and its seven-day wait are designed, not built.
  • The status is what the referrer says. It is not pulled from company systems.

The open questions are the costs listed under Key Design Changes: the seven-day wait, the missing reminder, false hope, and whether real referrers will set a status for a stranger.

Reflections

Most of the work was deciding who is allowed to say what, and what the product may assume when nobody says anything. A working prototype caught things a mockup would not, like a hug animation that read as romantic. AI helped with background research and with building from my spec. The decisions were mine.
